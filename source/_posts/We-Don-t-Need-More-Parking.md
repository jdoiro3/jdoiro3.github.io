I recently attended my wife's sister's graduation at the [Harang Auditorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warren_J._Harang_Jr._Municipal_Auditorium) in Thibodaux, Louisiana. It had been raining almost nonstop all day. Some roads were beginning to flood near the auditorium as the graduates dodged puddles and cars on their way inside. Since the parking lots bordering the auditorium were already filled, many attendees were stuck driving around searching for an open spot. Some resorted to parking across a busy boulevard, with no crosswalks for a safe crossing. Thankfully, a lone police officer stood in the road directing traffic and letting people cross. We ended up parking behind a vacant building's old parking lot and walked through an Aldi parking lot to get to the auditorium. We were planning on walking from home and avoid the hassle of parking. An easy less than a half mile walk, which we've done before, but given the rain, the fact it's not an enjoyable walk given the lack of sidewalks and crosswalks, we decided to drive. Searching for parking ourselves and watching many others do the same thing, one could easily think the area needs more parking, [something local officials are considering for the auditorium](https://www.lafourchegazette.com/announcements/mayor-addresses-chamber-breakfast-about-new-roadway-bridge-project-updates/article_8a9d9f1c-6903-482c-95ff-4439a0f789c9.html); but, when you look from a distance you realize the entire area is predominantly devoted to parking already.

![todo](/images/parking-lots-thibodaux.jpg)

The Harang Auditorium has a capacity of 3,500. Assuming everyone drives to the auditorium and most people carpool (each car brings 3 people on average), it would require around 1,166 parking spaces for mostly large trucks and SUVs. I used Google Earth to try and get a good estimate of the current number of spaces around the auditorium, including the large lots across the boulevard technically only for businesses in the strip. Counting all these spots yielded 700, a 40% deficit. Having "enough" parking for the auditorium would either require devoting even more land use to parking lots or building a very costly parking garage. [The average cost of a parking garage per spot is between $25,000-$55,000](https://smartconstructs.com/parking-garage-cost/). Adding more parking will likely just increase demand for parking, decrease the walkability of the area even more, and not really solve the problem, which is: how can *people* easily travel to and from the auditorium? The only safe option today is by car, given the cities lack of good urban planning and funding of an existing, almost unusable, [bus system](https://www.nicholls.edu/parking/good-earth-transit-bus-route/) (good luck finding the bus times), and the lack of safe pedestrian and bike routes in the area. A landscape which once was dominated by sugarcane fields, harvested, grinded and milled by locals to produce raw sugar and molasses, providing agricultural and economic benefit to the community, is now dominated by a concrete wasteland, with occasional businesses in-between, dangerous for humans, and who's sole purpose is to serve the car. One might think this is a natural evolution. A sort of metamorphosis, caused by natural economic forces driving each business to have their own, separate, parking lot. This isn't the case. The core driving force for the current parking environment, and as a consequence the urban landscape in places like Thibodaux and throughout America is Government mandated minimum parking requirements.

![todo](/images/parking-mandates.jpg)

[Thibodaux has minimum parking requirements](https://library.municode.com/la/thibodaux/codes/code_of_ordinances?nodeId=COOR_APXAZOOR_ARTVIIOREPALO_S704REOREVEPASP). Let's say Sally, a local resident, wants to open a pet grooming business here, she'd have to provide 1 free space per 350 square feet of GFA (ground floor area). If that parking didn't already exist, she'd be on the hook for building more parking. What if she'd somehow could open her pet grooming operation near a neighborhood, where most of your clientele could walk? She'd probably get a lot of business just by being in an area where dog walkers are common. First off, that would be next to impossible with the current cities zoning laws, but if she'd was friends with the mayor and could find this unicorn location in a walkable place, she'd probably still have to foot the bill for parking. The logical conclusion is that she'd end up building some parking spaces and add it as an implicit cost to her customers. This is a hypothetical scenario, I'll give you that, so a more concrete (pun intended) example is the land [Nicholls State University donated land to the Center For Traditional Louisiana Boat Building & Museum](https://traditionallouisianaboatbuilding.org/future-location), which my wife's grandfather co-founded in 1979.

![Tom Butler](/images/tom-butler.jpg)

Being a non-profit doesn't exempt you from the minimum parking requirements. So, not only are they raising funds for the actual structure that will house the historic boats and museum's facility, but they'll be required to foot the bill for parking. The site is directly across a highway from the University, so theoretically people could easily utilize existing parking and walk to the museum, assuming other measures could be taken by the city to make the museum accessible on foot. It's an extra burden put on the boat center, an organization doing amazing work preserving Louisiana's history. This is my first point: *minimum parking laws are bad for business*. They're not only bad for business but disproportionally impact local/small businesses relative to corporations, whom have ample capital to build as much parking as a local cities laws require. Removing such requirements promotes small businesses, while also allowing for better land usage.

> Rational people quickly become emotional about parking, and staunch conservatives turn into ardent communists.

-- Donald Shoup
